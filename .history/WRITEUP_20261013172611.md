company, one column per quarter. `orders.csv` has 20 rows, `shipments.csv` has 35. The revenue file
isn't tidy: "quarter" should be one column, but it's spread across 12 column headers, and each row
holds 12 observations instead of 1. `orders` and `shipments` are each tidy on their own — one row per
order, and one row per shipment (repeated `order_id`s in `shipments` are expected, since an order can
have multiple shipments). The raw-form charts in `output/` show the revenue data plotted by manually
transposing it, plus a histogram of order amounts and shipment counts per order.

**Task 1 — Reshape.** The revenue table broke tidy rule 1 — quarter is one variable, but it was spread
across 12 columns instead of living in one. `melt(id_vars="company", var_name="quarter",
value_name="revenue")` fixes that: it turns those 12 columns into a single `quarter` column and a
`revenue` column, giving one row per company-quarter pair — 60 rows total.

**Task 2 — Duplicate keys.** `order_id` is unique in `orders.csv` (20 of 20), but not in
`shipments.csv` (35 rows, only 20 distinct order IDs — some orders shipped in 2 or 3 parts). So
`order_id` is a primary key in `orders` but just a foreign key in `shipments`, and joining the two is
one-to-many: every order gets duplicated once per shipment.

**Task 3 — Join and compare means.** Joining `orders` to `shipments` gives 35 rows instead of 20,
since multi-shipment orders repeat. Averaging `amount` per customer on that joined table quietly
overweights customers whose orders had more shipments — customer C107's mean comes out to 106.0 on the
joined table, versus the correct 96.5 computed on `orders` alone. **The mean from `orders` alone is
right.** `amount` belongs to the order, not the shipment, so it should only be counted once per order —
joining double- or triple-counts it for any order with multiple shipments.

**Task 4 — Analysis-ready table(s).** The tidy revenue table (company × quarter) and the orders/shipments
table (order × shipment) don't share a key — nothing in the data maps a `company` name to a
`customer_id`. Combining them into one table would mean inventing a relationship that isn't there, so
instead I kept two separate tidy tables, each at its own grain, with no leftover index columns:
`revenue_analysis_ready.csv` (company, quarter, revenue) and `orders_analysis_ready.csv` (order_id,
customer_id, amount, tracking_number).

**Task 5 — Visualization, before vs. after.** In Task 0, plotting the revenue data meant transposing
the wide table and setting `company` as the index just to get quarters on the x-axis. After tidying,
it's one line: `sns.lineplot(data=revenue_final, x="quarter", y="revenue", hue="company")`, with the
legend built automatically from the `company` column. No manual reshaping needed, and the same code
keeps working if more companies or quarters get added later.
